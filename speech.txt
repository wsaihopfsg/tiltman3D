Good morning, everyone! It’s great to see all of you. 

I’ve decided to use my time today to talk about a "disability" that none of you have. That is, for being very short. Smaller-sized females are often described "petite" or "cute". Short male species are just, SHORT, in CAPS.  

Let's start from the very beginning. My parents named me Sai Ho. Now, for those of you who speak Cantonese, you already know the punchline. "Sai Ho" <strong><em>sounds exactly like</em></strong> "Small Size." I have always been shorter than everyone else. When I was in kindergarten, I made my parents call me Dai Ho, where Dai is the exact opposite of Sai. You can't blame me, look at Kah Loong, he is so LONG! 

Being this height comes with a very unique set of daily challenges. Supermarket runs are basically extreme sports. If the Lee Kum Kee premium soy sauce is on the top shelf at NTUC, I just have to accept that my family is consuming in-house brands — unless I want to use my qinggong to <strong><em>stand on the lowest shelf</em></strong>, which often does not help. Once, an NTUC <strong><em>staff told me</em></strong>, "Uncle, next time please ask for help!" 

Then there are the physical misunderstandings. Because of my frame and thanks the increasing number of tomboys, the cai png stall owner sometimes mistakes me for a female and calls me xiaojie. 

The conventional wisdom is that I am technically "too short to date." If I put my real height on Tinder or OkCupid, I am sure I will get zero match. But I consider myself an overachiever. I ended up marrying a wonderful woman who is actually taller than me. When <strong><em>we walk together</em></strong>, I just assume everyone thinks she’s a very generous celebrity doing charity work. 

Just like most Singaporean male, National Service has left deep impacts. When it was time for my pre-enlistment medical check-up, and I had a devastating realization: I was exactly 7 centimeters too tall to be exempted from National Service. Seven centimeters! Should have <strong><em>slouched my back</em></strong> when my height was taken.

Somehow, I made it into Officer Cadet School. For the majority of you who do not have to serve, OCS is one of the most physically and mentally demanding courses in the Singapore Armed Forces. This was taken in <strong><em>Taiwan</em></strong>. Can you find me? Sai Ho <strong><em>wave your hand!</em></strong> In fact Kenneth was my OCS senior, but he is normal - normal height, normal looks, normal intelligence. I faced many more challenges that as a shorter-than-usual cadet. The <strong><em>low wall</em></strong> was actually very high for me! I was at Brunei airport for our jungle training, I saw some Aussie soldiers. They were—OMG— three times my size! It was lucky that we were not <strong><em>wearing camouflage uniforms</em></strong> at the airport, otherwise I could have been mistaken as a child soldier.

I also have fond memories of using the 84mm recoilless rifle. When I adopted the <i>firing position</i>, my CO exclaimed, "The 84 is as long as you!" I powered through, thanks to the help of my platoon mates and my officers, and eventually made it to my Commissioning Parade. I went to the logistics store to loan my ceremonial No. 1 uniform. We searched the entire inventory. Not a single uniform <strong><em>fit my height</em></strong>. Luckily, the uniform is worn tucked-out, and I just made a fold to <strong><em>shorten my pants</em></strong>. As you can see, there is nothing I can do with my scabbard which is almost touching the floor. I hope there was a kid-sized sword. At my commissioning parade, <strong><em>I told my friends</em></strong> "Just look for a dent at the centre of the formation, that's me!"

Another embarrassing episode happened at church. I was chosen to be a pallbearer for a <strong><em>funeral service</em></strong>. We were in formation, all ready for the coffin to be put on our shoulders. The undertaker-in-charge saw me, and quickly shooed me away, and put one of his guys in my place. Later he explained that due to my height, the coffin <strong><em>weight distribution</em></strong> will not be even and at the worse case the coffin may topple over!   

The most humbling experience, however, happened outside of uniform. I was having a night-out at Hooters over at Clarke Quay. I went to the restroom, and a Caucasian guy stepped up to the urinal right next to mine. He peeped as I was peeing, and <strong><em>did this gesture</em></strong>. I smiled in embarrassment and exited quickly. But ang moh dua kee was real.

But it’s not all bad! Being vertically challenged has its perks. For one, I still get ID checked when I <strong><em>order a drink</em></strong> sometimes. Do you have any idea how incredible it feels to be a 49-year-old father of two to be checked if I was legal to drink? 

And let me add a few other minor things you tall people take for granted. <strong><em>In group photos</em></strong>, I am always right in front, surrounded by ladies. When I fly, my knees NEVER touch the seat in front of me. Every seat has extra leg room! 

Lastly, I want to share with you a book chapter that I wrote on Interference Mitigation in LTE. Yes, it's the shortest chapter in the whole book. No surprise there.

Thank you! I am HK Wong, from Hong Kong!
